## Topics

- The Clean Air Act structure: criteria pollutants, NAAQS, primary and secondary standards
- Attainment, nonattainment, and maintenance areas, and what changes between them
- Transportation conformity: the plan level and the project level
- Project-level hot-spot analysis, which pollutants it covers and which it does not
- The screening ladder: categorical finding, then screening model, then refined modeling
- Compliance as a floor rather than a goal

## Expectations

After this topic a student should be able to:

- Explain what a NAAQS is and what it does and does not regulate
- Determine whether a given project in a given area triggers a project-level analysis
- Explain why a hot-spot analysis exists for some pollutants and not others
- Articulate what a finding of no violation does and does not establish
- Identify a case where the legally required analysis is not the analysis that matters

## Prerequisites

**Conceptual.** Primary versus secondary pollutants, because the pollutant's formation mechanism
is why the regulatory treatment differs. Basic awareness of what a transportation project is.

**What students need to be able to do.** Nothing quantitative. This card is entirely conceptual,
which is part of why it reaches audiences who cannot be asked to calculate, and why it is a good
entry point for a policy or planning audience.

## What students get wrong here

- **They read compliance as safety.** A concentration below a standard is a legal finding, not a
  statement that no one is harmed. The distinction matters enormously and students rarely arrive
  with it.
- **They assume every project gets an air quality analysis.** Most do not, and understanding why
  is more instructive than memorizing the cases that do.
- **They conflate the plan level and the project level** of conformity. These answer different
  questions at different scales.
- **They expect ozone to have a hot-spot analysis.** It does not, and the reason is chemistry
  rather than policy oversight. This is a good moment to make the primary-versus-secondary
  distinction pay off.
- **They think a categorical finding is a shortcut.** It is a screening tier with its own basis;
  passing it is a result, not a waiver.

## What changes by audience

**Anchor points.** A planner enters here first, before any modeling, because the regulatory
question is the one their job actually poses. An environmental engineering audience enters after
the modeling, as the reason the modeling exists. A community audience enters here too, since
"was this required, and what did it show" is the question people actually ask at a meeting.

**Bridges.**
- Transportation and planning: conformity is a project-delivery gate, which sits in the process
  they already navigate alongside environmental documents and public involvement.
- Electrical engineering: a standard with an averaging time is a specification with a measurement
  condition attached. They understand that a spec means nothing without the test method.
- Public health: the standard-setting process is a risk-management decision informed by
  epidemiology, which is their field's output entering policy.

**Depth options.** The treatments below reflect the source courses. Select and combine
them according to the accepted lesson objectives, prerequisites, and time. Technical detail may be
central to a graduate lesson. Suggested cuts below apply to the source teaching context and are
overridden by the lesson plan's explicit cut order.

**Depth ladder.**
- *Awareness*: what the standards are, who sets them, what attainment means, what triggers an
  analysis.
- *Working*: walk a specific project through the decision tree and justify the outcome.
- *Technical*: the regulatory text itself, the applicability thresholds, and how a screening tier
  was derived.

**Suggested cut for the source lesson:** the history of the Clean Air Act, and the full list of criteria pollutants with
their values.
**Suggested content to protect in the source lesson:** that compliance is a floor, and that the required analysis and the important
analysis can be different things.

## Worked example

For an actual regulatory analysis, supply verified designation, project details, and applicable
rules (REG-01/03), then ask students to explain the decision path and its limits. Do not infer
all project obligations from a generic attainment statement.

For a hypothetical beyond-compliance exercise, explicitly adopt HYP-04, then ask what additional
analysis students would recommend and why. Provide evidence or stipulated conditions relevant to
their judgment. A local-versus-regional recommendation is a conclusion to justify, not a required
answer. DER-03 records why the imported ozone-significance argument remains unsupported.

## Notes and caveats

**Everything specific in this card has an expiry date.** Standards are revised, designations
change, and guidance is reissued. Any generated material must cite the version and date of what
it relied on. A card reviewer should treat an uncited regulatory claim as a defect.

Contested questions should distinguish evidence, uncertainty, and decision criteria. Worked
recommendations may demonstrate supported reasoning; assessment criteria should accept different
defensible conclusions rather than require the instructor's preferred position.
