> Inlet design considerations
> • Sample air should be brought into the laboratory through a vertical 
> stack.
> • Inlet that is high enough above ground level to minimize local 
> influences (a minimum height of 2 m above the roof, even higher 
> inlets may be required for obstructed sites).
> • If possible, the sampling duct should be brought through the roof of 
> the laboratory. If this is not possible, then a gradual bend in the inlet 
> pipe is acceptable.
> • High aerosol transmission efficiency that does not vary with wind 
> direction or wind speed. This can be achieved with a vertical air duct 
> with a cover that excludes drizzle, rain and snow mounted on top of 
> the inlet duct.
> • The inlet should have a particle cut-off diameter corresponding to 
> sampling goal under ambient conditions.
> 16

> Tubing and flow splitters
> • For the sampling of particles, the pipes should be made of conductive 
> material, and electrically grounded.
> • Short pieces of tubing might be replaced by conductive silicone tubing, 
> which is elastic and conducting at the same time.
> • A perfect inlet installation also avoids sources of turbulence (bends, 
> connectors) as best as possible (turbulence enhances particle losses 
> due to diffusion) and keeps the sampling lines as short as possible.
> • Isokinetic flow splitter: a sample is removed from the core of the main 
> aerosol flow rather than from streamlines near the wall of the main 
> pipe. This ensures a representative sampling especially of coarse and 
> nano-particles.
> 17

> Sample drying
> • Dry sampling maintains the RH at a sufficiently low
